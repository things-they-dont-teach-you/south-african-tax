# Lesson 1: How tax works in South Africa
<!-- facts: Income tax brackets; Rebates; Tax thresholds -->

## By the end, the learner can

- Say what SARS is and what the tax year is.
- Explain the difference between income tax, VAT and other taxes they already pay.
- Work out roughly how much income tax someone pays, using brackets and the primary rebate.
- Say why "moving into a higher bracket" never makes you take home less.

## Key ideas

**SARS** (South African Revenue Service) collects tax for the government. You deal with it through
**eFiling** (website) or the **SARS MobiApp**.

**The tax year** runs from 1 March to the end of February, and is named after the year it ends.
See `facts.md` for the current one.

**You already pay tax.** VAT (15%) is in almost every price. Fuel levy is in petrol. Income tax is
the one this course is mostly about: tax on what you earn.

**Taxable income** is what you earn, minus allowed deductions (lesson 4).

**Brackets are marginal.** Each rate only applies to the slice of income inside that bracket.
Moving into a higher bracket only taxes the extra rands at the higher rate, so earning more always
means taking home more.

**Rebates** reduce your tax, rand for rand. Everyone gets the primary rebate, which is why
low earners pay no income tax (the tax threshold in `facts.md`).

## Worked example (2027 tax year)

Thandi is 30 and has a taxable income of R300 000.

1. R300 000 falls in the 245 101 – 383 100 bracket: R44 118 + 26% of (300 000 − 245 100).
2. 26% of R54 900 = R14 274. Tax before rebates = R58 392.
3. Minus the primary rebate of R17 820 = **R40 572 for the year**, about R3 381 a month.
4. That is about 13.5% of her income, even though her top bracket is 26%. Her *average* rate is
   much lower than her *marginal* rate.

## Exercise

Ask the learner to work out the tax on R200 000, then on R400 000, using `facts.md`. Then ask: why
is the average rate always below the top bracket rate?
